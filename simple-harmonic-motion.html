Simple Harmonic Motion

Push a child on a swing and let go — it doesn't just stop, it swings back and forth, slower and slower, until it settles. That back-and-forth motion, repeating in a predictable rhythm, is the idea behind Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM).

Here's the key insight: in SHM, something always pulls the object back toward its resting position — and the harder you push it away from that position, the harder it gets pulled back. A guitar string, a mass on a spring, the swing, even the way your phone's speaker cone vibrates to make sound — all of these follow this same simple rule.

Why it matters: almost every wave, every vibration, every oscillating signal in electronics eventually gets analyzed by breaking it down into simple harmonic pieces. Understanding SHM is the doorway to understanding waves, sound, light, and AC circuits.

Definition

A particle executes Simple Harmonic Motion when the restoring force acting on it is always directed toward a fixed equilibrium point, and is directly proportional to its displacement from that point.

F = −kx

Here, F is the restoring force, x is the displacement from equilibrium, and k is the force constant. The negative sign shows the force always opposes the displacement — it always pulls the object back.

The Equation of Motion

Using Newton's second law, F = ma, and substituting a = d²x/dt²:

d²x/dt² + ω²x = 0

where ω² = k/m. This is the standard differential equation of SHM. Its general solution is:

x(t) = A sin(ωt + φ)

where A is the amplitude (maximum displacement), ω is the angular frequency, and φ is the phase constant that depends on where the motion starts.

Key Characteristics

  • Time Period (T): time for one complete oscillation, T = 2π/ω
  • Frequency (f): oscillations per second, f = 1/T
  • Velocity: v = Aω cos(ωt + φ), maximum at the equilibrium position
  • Acceleration: a = −Aω² sin(ωt + φ), maximum at the extreme positions

Solved Example

A particle in SHM has amplitude 5 cm and time period 2 s. Find its maximum velocity.

ω = 2π/T = 2π/2 = π rad/s
vmax = Aω = 5 × π = 15.7 cm/s

Your syllabus stops at the ideal spring-mass system, but the same F = −kx pattern shows up everywhere once you start looking:

  • Quartz watches and clocks: a quartz crystal is cut so that it vibrates in SHM at a precise frequency (32,768 Hz) when a voltage is applied — that steady vibration is literally what your watch counts to keep time.
  • Seismographs: the pendulum inside a seismograph is a SHM system tuned so that ground vibrations from an earthquake get recorded as a wave pattern.
  • AC electrical circuits: the voltage in an LC (inductor-capacitor) circuit oscillates with the exact same equation as a mechanical spring — energy just swaps between the capacitor's electric field and the inductor's magnetic field instead of between kinetic and potential energy.
  • Molecular vibrations: atoms bonded in a molecule vibrate around their equilibrium separation approximately like tiny SHM oscillators — this is the basis of infrared spectroscopy, used to identify chemical compounds.

Energy in SHM

Total mechanical energy stays constant, continuously exchanging between kinetic and potential:

E = ½kA² = ½mω²A²

At the extreme positions, all energy is potential; at equilibrium, all energy is kinetic. This constant total is exactly why an ideal SHM oscillator, once started, never stops — real systems lose energy to friction/resistance, which leads to damped oscillation (a topic worth exploring next).

Where SHM Breaks Down

Real oscillators are only approximately harmonic. Push a pendulum too far and F = −kx stops holding — this is why SHM formulas for a pendulum only work for small angles (below about 15°). Beyond that, you're dealing with nonlinear oscillation, which needs more advanced mathematics (elliptic integrals) to solve exactly.

The Bigger Picture

SHM is the linear approximation of almost any system sitting in a stable equilibrium. Near any minimum of a potential energy curve, the potential looks approximately parabolic — and a parabolic potential always produces SHM. This is why the same math appears in mechanical springs, electrical circuits, molecular bonds, and even in quantum mechanics (the quantum harmonic oscillator is one of the few problems solvable exactly in Schrödinger's equation, and forms the basis for understanding photon and phonon behavior).
